Opioid prescribing

Day 1 post-laparotomy, pain 8/10 — now what?

Ward round done, you're holding the drug chart. Before reaching for morphine, build the base of the ladder properly.

Step 1 — non-opioid, always regular
Paracetamol 1 g QDS regular, not PRN (reduce if <50 kg or significant liver disease). NSAIDs only if no renal impairment, no GI bleed risk, and the surgical team is happy — many post-op surgical patients fail at least one of those.
Step 2 — weak opioid
Codeine 30–60 mg QDS (max 240 mg/day ≈ only ~24 mg oral morphine — it's weaker than people think; BNF caps acute use at 3 days). Up to ~1 in 10 people are CYP2D6 poor metabolisers who get little analgesia from it. Stop it when you start a strong opioid — never run both.
Step 3 — strong opioid
Morphine first line. Immediate-release (oramorph) to find the dose, modified-release to hold it. And a surgical reflex worth building: pain out of proportion or escalating = ask why (leak, ischaemia, compartment, retention) — don't just up-titrate.
Opioid prescribing

Starting morphine — the numbers

Opioid-naïve adult
Oramorph 5–10 mg up to 4-hourly (BNF acute pain: 10 mg every 4 h; halve it in the elderly or frail — on surgical wards most start at 5 mg and titrate). Needing more than 2–3 PRN doses a day? The background needs sorting, not more PRNs.
Converting to modified-release
Add up 24 h of use → give as MR morphine (Zomorph/MST) split BD. Example: 30 mg used in 24 h → MR 15 mg BD.
The 1/6 rule for breakthrough
PRN dose = 1/10 to 1/6 of the total daily background dose, given up to 2–4-hourly. Recalculate it every time the background changes.
What you co-prescribe, every time
A regular laxative (constipation is near-universal and doesn't wear off) + a PRN antiemetic (nausea usually settles in days). Forgetting the laxative is the classic F1 letter-of-complaint.

Conversions you'll actually use

The four to memorise (from oral morphine)
  • Codeine → morphine: ÷ 10
  • Oral morphine → oral oxycodone: ÷ 1.5–2 (use ÷2 and titrate — safer)
  • Oral morphine → s/c morphine: ÷ 2
  • Oral morphine → s/c diamorphine: ÷ 3
When switching drug or route, err low and use PRNs to catch up — you can always give more, you can't take it back.

Opioid prescribing

Staying safe (and keeping your patients safe)

Toxicity — what kills is respiratory depression
Sedation comes before respiratory depression — a drowsy patient on opioids is a warning, not a comfort. RR <8 or unrousable → ABCDE, stop opioids, give naloxone per trust protocol. In a patient on therapeutic opioids the BNF low-dose regimen is 100–200 micrograms IV, then 100-microgram steps — enough to restore breathing without stripping all analgesia; the 400-microgram+ escalating doses are for overdose. Naloxone is short-acting — watch for re-sedation and repeat.
Renal impairment
eGFR <30: morphine metabolites accumulate → switch to oxycodone (reduced dose). Severe/dialysis: fentanyl or alfentanil, with senior/palliative advice. This is also why you re-check opioid charts in every AKI — the two topics tonight are the same patient.
Discharge scripts (controlled drugs)
CD prescriptions need formulation, strength, dose, and total quantity in words and figures. Send home the smallest sensible supply with a clear weaning plan — post-op opioids are for weeks, not months. Advise not to drive while drowsy or after dose changes.
AKI

Spot it and stage it (KDIGO)

AKI = creatinine rise ≥26 µmol/L within 48 h, or ≥1.5× baseline within 7 days, or urine output <0.5 ml/kg/h for 6 h. Stage by the worst criterion.

Stage 1
Creatinine 1.5–1.9× baseline (or ≥26 rise) · UO <0.5 ml/kg/h for 6–12 h
Stage 2
Creatinine 2.0–2.9× baseline · UO <0.5 ml/kg/h for ≥12 h
Stage 3
Creatinine ≥3× baseline, or ≥354 µmol/L, or started on renal replacement · UO <0.3 ml/kg/h for ≥24 h or anuria ≥12 h

Know the baseline — a creatinine of 110 can be a bad AKI in a patient whose baseline is 55. And on a surgical ward, think causes in three columns: pre-renal (hypovolaemia, sepsis) · renal (drugs, contrast) · post-renal (blocked catheter, retention, ureteric injury).

AKI

The AKI bundle — do all of it, same day

1 · Assess volume & sepsis
Fluid status examination, fluid balance chart, NEWS. Hypovolaemic → fluid challenge and reassess. Septic → Sepsis 6 now.
2 · Dip the urine
Urinalysis on every AKI, documented. Blood + protein → think intrinsic renal disease and discuss.
3 · Medication review
The drug chart is the commonest fixable cause — next slide is exactly this.
4 · Exclude obstruction
Palpable bladder? Catheter blocked? Bladder scan. Renal USS within 24 h if the cause isn't obvious — sooner if pyonephrosis is suspected (that's an emergency, later slide).
5 · Bloods & monitoring
VBG for a fast K⁺, repeat U&E, strict fluid balance, daily weight (most trust AKI bundles add a catheter + hourly urine output from stage 2 — that trigger is local practice, not NICE).
6 · Escalate
Stage 3, oliguria despite filling, K⁺ ≥6.0, or no clear cause → senior + renal/critical care early. Don't sit on a rising creatinine overnight.

LIVEQuestion 4 — the K⁺ is back…

The NBT numbers — Hyperkalaemia SOP (Aug 2024, UKKA 2023)
  • Bands: mild 5.5–5.9 · moderate 6.0–6.4 · severe ≥6.5 = emergency treatment. ECG + cardiac monitor from K⁺ ≥6.0; exclude pseudohyperkalaemia (repeat in a lithium-heparin tube).
  • Protect the heart (if ECG changes or K⁺ ≥6.5): 30 ml 10% calcium gluconate IV over 10 min or 10 ml 10% calcium chloride over 5 min (6.8 mmol) — large vein, repeat ECG, further dose after 5 min if changes persist.
  • Shift: 10 units soluble insulin in 25 g glucose over 15–30 min (check glucose first; if <7.0 → 10% glucose at 50 ml/h for 5 h) ± salbutamol 10–20 mg nebulised.
  • Remove: sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (Lokelma) 10 g TDS up to 72 h (stop when K⁺ ≤5.0) · consider dialysis if K⁺ ≥6.5 despite treatment.
  • ECG changes to name: peaked T waves, flat/absent P waves, broad QRS, sine wave, bradycardia, VT.
  • Hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest (same SOP, arrest-only page): ALS + IV calcium bolus (10 ml 10% chloride or 30 ml 10% gluconate, repeat if no ROSC in 5–10 min) + insulin–glucose bolus + sodium bicarbonate 50 ml 8.4% IV — no ROSC → dialysis/ECMO. Contact the renal team early whenever dialysis is even being considered.
When does an AKI need dialysis? — A·E·I·O·U
Acidosis (refractory, pH <7.15) · Electrolytes (refractory K⁺ ≥6.5) · Intoxicants (certain poisonings) · Overload (fluid, refractory) · Uraemia (pericarditis, encephalopathy). These are the words that get ICU/renal moving on the phone. (NICE lists the domains without numbers — the pH/K⁺ cut-offs are renal-ICU convention.)

UGI anatomy

The coeliac trunk — tap a dot, name the vessel

Three branches at T12 → left gastric · splenic · common hepatic. Lesser curve: left + right gastric. Greater curve: the two gastro-omentals. Fundus: short gastrics from the splenic.

Duodenum in 20 seconds
Four parts; D1 is the ulcer zone (the gastroduodenal artery lies directly behind it). D2 holds the ampulla — where foregut becomes midgut, and why coeliac trunk hands over to SMA. D3 crosses under the SMA in front of the aorta.

Why anatomy = the operation (30 seconds)
Right hemicolectomy → ileocolic ± right colic. Extended right → + middle colic. Left hemicolectomy → left colic. Anterior resection → IMA (± high/low tie). The resection follows the vessel, and the vessel carries the lymph nodes — that's the whole logic of cancer surgery. (You met these names in the terminology module.)

Bonus · Urology on-call

Night shift survival — tap a scenario

🚫 Acute urinary retention

  • Painful, palpable bladder → bladder scan → catheterise (14–16 Ch), document the residual, send a CSU.
  • Treat causes: constipation, opioids/anticholinergics, UTI, clots. Plan the TWOC before you forget.
  • Residual >1 L + renal impairment = high-pressure chronic retention → watch for post-obstructive diuresis (>200 ml/h for 2 h or more): monitor, replace ~50% IV, U&Es.

🧱 Catheter won't pass / blocked / bypassing

  • Won't pass: plenty of lidocaine gel + time, then a 16 Ch (larger and stiffer — not smaller). Never force. Failed twice → urology, don't traumatise the urethra.
  • Never inflate the balloon without urine draining.
  • Blocked: flush 50 ml saline; still blocked → change it. Bypassing with a patent catheter is usually bladder spasm — and check for constipation.

🩸 Clot retention / frank haematuria

  • Clot retention needs a 3-way catheter (20–22 Ch) + washout until clear, then irrigation — a standard catheter will just block.
  • FBC, G&S, review anticoagulants, cause needs follow-up (tumour until proven otherwise in the older patient) → urology.

🔒 Paraphimosis

  • Retracted foreskin stuck behind the glans → swells. Analgesia, firm compression, reduce. Can't reduce → urology (dorsal slit).
  • Commonest iatrogenic cause: catheterising and forgetting to pull the foreskin back down. Make it a habit.

LIVEQuestion 7

⚠️ Never miss #1 — testicular torsion

Sudden severe unilateral pain ± vomiting; high-riding, tender testis. Clinical diagnosis — do not wait for imaging. Salvage ~90% under 6 h. NBM + urology immediately.

⚠️ Never miss #2 — infected obstructed kidney

Fever/sepsis + loin pain + hydronephrosis or a known stone = pus under pressure. Antibiotics alone will not fix it — needs urgent decompression (nephrostomy or stent). Sepsis 6 + urology now, tonight.
